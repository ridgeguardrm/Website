01Tail risk

Markets move in jumps. Most allocation models assume they don’t.

The normal distribution sits underneath a great deal of conventional portfolio construction. It is convenient, and it fails in exactly the region that decides whether a family’s capital compounds or is set back a decade: the left tail. The chart shows every trading day since October 2007, standardised, on a logarithmic scale so the extremes are visible.

Excess kurtosis

15.2

A normal distribution scores 0. Higher values mean more of the risk lives in rare, large moves.

Days beyond four standard deviations

38 observed  ·  0.3 expected under a normal model, over 4,660 trading days.

How rare is a crash, really?

The three worst days in each market, and how often two different models say they should happen. The heavy-tailed answer is the one that matches what investors lived through.

03The mandate

What working with RidgeGuard includes.

We work alongside your existing managers and custodians. You keep control of execution; we supply the allocation, the policy around it, and an honest account of the risk you are carrying.

  1. i

    Strategic allocation design

    Target weights and permitted ranges built around your return objective, liquidity needs, concentration limits and tax position. Risk is measured in the tail directly, not inferred from volatility.

    You receive: an allocation memo with ranges, rationale and the scenarios it was stress-tested against.

  2. ii

    Rebalancing governance

    A written policy for when to trade, how far weights may drift and what each rebalance costs after tax. Every candidate policy is tested out-of-sample before it is adopted.

    You receive: a policy document your committee can approve, and a trade list when a trigger is hit.

  3. iii

    Tail-risk reporting

    Expected shortfall, drawdown, and tail-adjusted return measures at portfolio and holding level, with regime context so a number is never read without its backdrop.

    You receive: a monthly risk report and a live dashboard login.

  4. iv

    Forward scenarios

    One-, two- and three-year ranges of outcomes from 10,000 simulated paths that keep the fat tails in, so planning conversations start from a distribution rather than a single forecast.

    You receive: percentile bands for wealth, drawdown and spending capacity.

04Foundations

Seventy years of research, and the standard regulators now use.

Nothing we do depends on a novel theory. It depends on taking the established one seriously. The case for heavy-tailed risk measurement has been building in the academic literature since the 1960s, and since 2016 it has been the basis on which banks hold capital against market risk.

1952

Portfolio selection

Markowitz frames diversification as a trade-off between expected return and risk. Journal of Finance

1963

Prices jump

Mandelbrot shows speculative price changes are far more extreme than a normal law allows. Journal of Business

1965

The evidence holds

Fama confirms fat tails across the Dow Jones constituents. Journal of Business

1966

Reward per unit of risk

Sharpe introduces the reward-to-variability ratio, now the industry’s common yardstick. Journal of Business

1988

Rebalancing as strategy

Perold and Sharpe show that how a portfolio is rebalanced shapes its payoff in rising and falling markets. Financial Analysts Journal

1991

Downside risk

Sortino and van der Meer separate harmful volatility from the upside an investor wants. Journal of Portfolio Management

1999

Coherent risk

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath set out the properties a sound risk measure must satisfy. Mathematical Finance

2000

Optimising the tail

Rockafellar and Uryasev make conditional value-at-risk practical to optimise. Journal of Risk

2005

Fat tails in practice

Rachev, Menn and Fabozzi bring heavy-tailed and skewed models into portfolio and risk management. Wiley Finance

2016

Expected shortfall becomes the rule

The Basel Committee’s market-risk framework replaces 99% value-at-risk with 97.5% expected shortfall for bank capital. BCBS, FRTB
